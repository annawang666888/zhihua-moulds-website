Garden & Landscape Concrete Moulds Guide

A practical guide to concrete moulds for landscape work: stepping stones and faux stone paving, decorative breeze blocks for screen walls, and tree rings and garden edging for planting beds.

Landscape and garden projects use a surprisingly wide range of precast concrete elements: the paving you walk on, the screen walls that give privacy, and the rings and edges that hold planting beds together. All three are cast in plastic moulds, and all three are chosen mainly on finished size, surface pattern and how the units combine on site.

This guide covers the three families most often bought together: stepping stone and faux stone paving moulds, breeze block and decorative screen block moulds, and tree ring and garden edging moulds.

1. Stepping stones and faux stone paving

Stepping stones are the entry point for most garden paving projects. Round discs in Ø20cm, Ø35cm and 50cm are set into lawn or gravel, usually cast 5–6cm thick. Because the mould face carries the finished texture, pattern definition matters more here than raw mould strength.

Faux stone moulds go further: they reproduce irregular natural stone texture in sizes from 5×5cm multi-cavity moulds up to 100×45cm slabs. Matte litchi-texture finishes are popular for patios because they stay slip-resistant when wet. For park and scenic walkways, wood-grain moulds cast 90–200cm strips that imitate timber decking without the maintenance.

Traditional Chinese pattern tiles — lotus, cloud, fret, coin and blessing motifs in 32×32 to 50×50cm — serve courtyard, temple and heritage-style paving. These sell on pattern catalogue depth rather than size range.

  • Ask for: finished slab length × width × thickness, plus a photo of the target texture.
  • Watch out for: whether units sit on sand, gravel, lawn or a mortar bed — this changes the thickness you need.
  • Cost lever: multi-cavity (1-out-9, 1-out-25) moulds cut casting time on small units dramatically.

2. Breeze blocks for screen walls and ventilated facades

Breeze blocks — also called decorative screen blocks, ventilation blocks or jali blocks — are perforated concrete units that build screen walls, privacy partitions, ventilated facades, garden walls and balcony infills. They give shade and airflow while carrying a strong architectural pattern, which is why they have come back into fashion in Australia, the US, the Middle East and Southeast Asia.

The dominant format is 20×20×6cm — the most widely specified screen block size, and where pattern choice matters most. 30×30×7cm gives a larger face and builds wall area faster, which suits feature walls. Intermediate and thick formats (25×25×6, 32×32×10, 34×34×6cm) cover square fret designs and structural screens, while 40×40×7cm handles high walls and facade panels.

  • Ask for: block face size, thickness, and a photo or drawing of the pattern.
  • Watch out for: pattern openness — a very open pattern looks good but weakens the wall and needs reinforcement planning.
  • Cost lever: stick to one face size across the wall so a single mould family covers the whole job.

3. Tree rings and garden edging

Tree rings (also called tree grates or tree pit surrounds) protect the root zone of street and park trees, hold soil in place, stop pedestrians compacting the pit and give the streetscape a finished edge. Garden edging stones do the same job for planting beds and lawn-to-paving transitions.

The important decision is one-piece ring versus modular set. A fixed decorative ring — sunburst, cast-iron-look, cloud or petal motifs in 50×50 to 64.5×64.5cm — looks best but only fits one pit size. A modular four-piece or eight-piece set uses repeated straight units plus corners, so changing how many straights go per side lets one mould kit build several pit diameters. On multi-size municipal projects that usually wins on total mould cost.

For irregular or large pits, straight, elbow and corner segments from 40cm up to 150cm long let you follow almost any outline.

  • Ask for: the finished tree pit opening (inner diameter or clear square) and the surround width.
  • Watch out for: whether the ring sits flush with paving or is raised as a kerb — the profile differs.
  • Cost lever: modular sets beat fixed rings whenever the project has more than two pit sizes.

4. Why these three are usually bought together

A single landscape contract often needs all three: paving for the paths, screen blocks for the boundary or pergola wall, and tree rings for the planted areas. Buying the moulds from one supplier keeps the concrete mix, colour and finish consistent across the site, and lets you consolidate everything into one container.

Mixed-SKU loading is normal for these products. A 20ft container can carry a working set of stepping stone, breeze block and tree ring moulds for a mid-size landscaping operation.

5. What to send for a fast quote

For any of the three families, the fastest path to an accurate quote is a photo of the target result plus finished dimensions. Specifically:

  • Paving: slab size, thickness, surface pattern photo, and whether multi-cavity is wanted.
  • Breeze blocks: block face size, thickness, pattern reference, and total wall area in square metres.
  • Tree rings: finished pit opening, surround width and height, flush or raised, and how many pit sizes the project has.
  • All: quantity, destination country, and whether patterns or SKUs will be mixed in one shipment.
